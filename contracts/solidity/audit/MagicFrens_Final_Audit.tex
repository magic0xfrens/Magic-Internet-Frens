\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The Cauldron is a self-perpetuating token launchpad built on Uniswap~v4: a hook meters
volume and collects an ETH fee, a registry summons a fixed-supply generation token into
a two-position pool (a tradeable \emph{active} band and an out-of-range \emph{reserve}
band that backs $1{:}1$ migration and an NFT redemption floor), a governor selects the
next brew, and a hook-native perpetuals engine writes real price impact into the same
pool. When volume dies, anybody may \code{relaunch()} and the machine is reborn.

This report is an independent, from-scratch review of all 47 in-scope Solidity files
(\input{zz_inventory_stats}\unskip). It was produced without reference to any prior
audit, report, exploit write-up or design document in the repository; every claim below
is re-derived from the sources and, wherever possible, demonstrated on a live Uniswap~v4
fork.

We report \textbf{22 findings}: 6 High, 8 Medium and 8 Low/Informational. Six of them are
demonstrated by executable proofs of concept. The most consequential are: a complete
bypass of the protocol's only revenue mechanism via exact-output sells; a liquidation
oracle that freezes whenever the perpetuals book is empty, so the next position opened is
liquidated inside its own opening transaction; an unbounded governor scan that lets a
single NFT holder permanently brick the rebirth for a few hundredths of an ETH; a
break-glass function that permanently strands an entire generation's liquidity and
bricks \code{relaunch()}; a governance handoff that irrecoverably burns every economic
setter; and --- specific to the stated deployment target --- a death clock denominated in
\code{block.number}, which on an Arbitrum Orbit chain lets any passer-by permanently
destroy a healthy, liquid token roughly an hour after launch.

All six High findings and three of the Medium findings have been \textbf{fixed} in this
engagement, and each proof of concept has been converted into a regression test that
asserts the exploit is now blocked. The suite stands at \textbf{302 passing tests across
57 suites}, including handler-based stateful invariant runs against live Uniswap~v4.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Independence}
This review was conducted under a hard independence constraint: no pre-existing audit
report, exploit write-up, design document, runbook or findings note in the repository was
read. Where the source contains inline references to earlier findings (for example
``audit A-01'', ``audit H-03''), those comments were treated as \emph{claims by the
authors to be verified}, not as inputs. Every finding below carries its own derivation
and, where the code cites a prior fix, we state independently whether that fix holds.

Two mechanical exceptions were unavoidable and are recorded for transparency: pre-existing
test files were edited to keep the suite compiling after the remediation (a two-line
idiom substitution applied by script), and file names in those directories appear in
build output. No assertion or narrative from them informed any finding.

\subsection{Method}
The system was read end to end before any finding was written, and modelled as a whole:
the cross-contract call graph (\S\ref{sec:arch}), the delegatecall facet sharing the
registry's storage, the Uniswap~v4 unlock/settle/take flows, the token lifecycle, the
reserve accounting, and the perpetuals risk engine. Analysis then proceeded in two
passes:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.4em,style=nextline]
\item[Pass A --- coverage] A complete inventory of externally reachable functions with
their access gates (\S\ref{sec:inventory}), and an explicit invariant register with a
per-invariant argument for or against (\S\ref{sec:invariants}).
\item[Pass B --- adversarial] For each custody boundary, an attempt to construct a
concrete exploit; where none exists, an explicit safety argument
(\S\ref{sec:failed}). Confirmed exploits were reduced to executable proofs of concept.
\end{description}

Testing ran against a \emph{pinned} Ethereum-Sepolia fork driving the real
\code{PoolManager}, \code{PositionManager} and canonical Permit2. Appendix~\ref{sec:harness}
records two harness defects discovered during this work that silently invalidate
time-dependent tests on this profile; both had to be fixed before several findings could
be observed at all.

\section{System Architecture}\label{sec:arch}

\subsection{Value topology}
Three custody domains hold essentially all protocol value:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{The pool positions.} A generation's entire 777{,}000{,}000-token supply
lives inside Uniswap~v4 as two positions owned by the registry: a full-range
\emph{active} position, and a single-sided \emph{reserve} position parked below the
launch tick (pure \code{token1} until the token appreciates roughly $69\times$). The
reserve is the sole backing for $1{:}1$ migration, the genesis redemption floor and the
collection legacy floors --- three claimants on one pot.
\item \textbf{The hook's ETH.} \code{relaunchETH}, \code{legacyBuffer} and
\code{proposerOwed} are three independently tracked pots inside \code{CauldronHook},
funded by the swap fee and spent on the next generation's liquidity, on token buybacks,
and on proposer rewards.
\item \textbf{The perpetuals vault.} \code{PerpEngine} holds depositor ETH
(\code{plv}), lent token inventory (\code{plvToken}), an insurance buffer, and a
segregated short-side yield pot.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The lifecycle}
\code{summon()} deploys a fixed-supply token (plain \code{CREATE}), initialises the pool,
seeds the two positions --- either atomically with a ``green-candle'' exact-output buy, or
progressively by handing the active tranche to \code{CauldronSeeder}, which streams it in
as a side effect of organic trading --- and deploys the brew's NFT collection. Trading
accrues fees, crystal credit and volume. When 24-hour volume falls below the threshold the
pool reads \emph{dead}, and \code{relaunch()} force-closes perpetuals, tears down the LP,
burns the recovered tokens, crystallises the dying collection's floor, launches the
governor's winner, and re-seeds. Old holders migrate $1{:}1$ by burning.

\subsection{The facet split}
\code{CauldronRegistry} sits 945 bytes under EIP-170 only because the OG-redemption
bodies were extracted into \code{RedemptionExt}, which the registry reaches by
\code{delegatecall} with the original calldata. Both derive from \code{CauldronBase} and
add \emph{no} state, so their layouts are identical by construction --- the crux of the
design. We verified this independently (\S\ref{sec:invariants}, INV-F); note in particular
that \code{poolManager}, \code{positionManager} and \code{hook} are storage variables
rather than immutables precisely because immutables resolve against the executing
contract's code and would read as zero inside the facet.

\section{L2 (Arbitrum Nitro / Orbit) Deployment Considerations}\label{sec:l2}

The protocol is written against Ethereum's execution semantics but is destined for the
``Robinhood'' chain, an Arbitrum Nitro/Orbit L2. The test suite runs against an
L1-like Sepolia fork and therefore cannot reproduce those semantics; the analysis below is
derived from Nitro's documented behaviour and, where it could be modelled, exercised by
advancing \code{block.number} and \code{block.timestamp} \emph{independently}.

\subsection{The governing fact: \code{block.number} is not an L2 block counter}
On Arbitrum Nitro, \code{block.number} reports the \emph{parent chain's} block number, not
the count of L2 blocks; it advances in jumps, and many L2 blocks share one value.
(\code{ArbSys.arbBlockNumber()} is the L2 counter.) The wall-clock meaning of any
block-denominated window is therefore a property of the settlement layer:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Constant (original)} & \textbf{Blocks} & \textbf{Orbit L2 ($\approx$12\,s parent)} & \textbf{Orbit L3 ($\approx$0.25\,s parent)} \\
\midrule
\code{BLOCKS\_PER\_HOUR} & 300 & 1\,h & 75\,s \\
\code{BLOCKS\_PER\_DAY} & 7\,200 & 24\,h & 30\,min \\
\code{snipeWindowBlocks} & 30 & $\approx$6\,min & $\approx$7.5\,s \\
\code{blockhash} horizon & 256 & $\approx$51\,min & $\approx$64\,s \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
This single fact drives finding Z-05 (\SevHigh) and residuals Z-13 and Z-14. Because the
two volume constants were declared \code{constant}, there was no setter and no migration
path, and \code{CauldronHook} had 58 bytes of EIP-170 headroom --- one could not have been
added. The remediation re-denominates the volume window in wall-clock seconds, which
carries the intended meaning identically on L1, on an Orbit L2 and on an Orbit L3.

\subsection{\code{block.timestamp}}
Nitro timestamps are the sequencer's clock at one-second granularity, non-decreasing and
bounded relative to L1. Every timestamp-keyed mechanism in the protocol therefore ports
cleanly: the emergency timelock, \code{minLifetime}, the governor's \code{VOTING\_PERIOD},
the vesting window, the TWAP window, \code{OBS\_INTERVAL}, funding $\Delta t$, and the
perpetuals warm-up. The engine's per-block liquidation throttle is already keyed on
\code{block.timestamp} with a correct in-source justification. One consequence deserves
recording: because that cap resets \emph{every second} rather than every 12 seconds, its
\emph{per-minute} bound is roughly $12\times$ looser than the L1 reading of the same
parameter, even though the atomic-cascade guarantee is unchanged (Z-10).

\subsection{\code{block.prevrandao}}
Constant on Arbitrum/Orbit. The protocol correctly does not rely on it for any live
randomness: the crystal gacha and both NFT reveals use commit-reveal over
\code{blockhash}, and the anti-snipe jitter folds in the live pool tick precisely because
\code{prevrandao} is useless there. The sole remaining use is
\code{MagicFrensPeg.\_random}, an out-of-build legacy contract (Z-15).

\subsection{\code{blockhash}}
Nitro documents \code{blockhash(x)} as returning a \emph{cryptographically insecure,
pseudo-random} value for $x$ in $[\,\code{block.number}-256,\ \code{block.number})$, zero
outside it, and explicitly \emph{not} the L1 block hash. Two consequences:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item Every commit in one parent-block window shares a seed. Per-ticket variation comes
from $\mathrm{keccak}(\textit{bh}, \textit{player}, \textit{batch}, \textit{index})$, so
outcomes remain distinct, but the entropy source is one documented-insecure value.
We record this as \emph{Suspected --- requires validation on the target chain}: whether
the value is predictable \emph{before} the window closes depends on the ArbOS derivation,
which we could not verify from the sources in scope.
\item The 256-block horizon collapses to roughly a minute on a fast parent chain, so seed
expiry stops being exceptional. The expiry path re-anchors and \code{break}s, which
head-of-line blocks the whole FIFO queue and hands the batch's owner a fresh roll
(Z-14, demonstrated).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{\code{gasleft()} and the 63/64 rule}
Nitro deducts the L1 data-posting cost up front and then meters execution on the ordinary
EVM schedule, so the hook's \emph{gas-capped} sub-calls port safely: each forwards
$\code{gasleft()}-R$ and keeps exactly $R$, so an out-of-gas child consumes precisely its
budget. The dangerous cases are the calls that are \emph{not} capped ---
\code{hook.forceClosePerps()} and \code{hook.resolveTickets(300)} inside
\code{relaunch()} --- where the 63/64 rule leaves the parent only $1/64$ of its gas and the
rebirth dies with it (Z-07).

\subsection{\code{tx.origin}}
Used for crystal attribution on untagged swaps and for liquidator credit. For ordinary L2
transactions this behaves as on L1. For L1$\to$L2 retryables the origin is the
\emph{aliased} L1 address, so a keeper reward can be credited to an address controlled only
from L1 (Z-21). The design note in the source correctly identifies the ERC-4337 caveat.

\subsection{Sequencer, mempool and reorgs}
A single sequencer with no public mempool removes classical mempool sandwiching, which
materially weakens the front-running threat against the anti-snipe window and the TWAP
mark. It does \emph{not} remove ordering games where the attacker can \emph{cause} the
event they wish to bracket --- notably the legacy buy-back, whose trigger condition
(\code{legacyBuffer >= legacyThreshold}) is publicly computable and can be tripped by the
attacker's own swap in the same transaction (Z-17, bounded by the buffer size).

\finding{Z-01 --- Exact-output sells pay zero hook fee and still mint crystal credit}
{\SevHigh}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Fixed}
{\code{CauldronHook.\_afterSwap} / \code{\_beforeSwap}}
{\code{test/attacks/Z01\_ExactOutSellFeeBypass.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} The hook takes its fee in ETH only. \code{\_beforeSwap} charges
exact-input buys; \code{\_afterSwap} charges whenever ETH is the \emph{unspecified}
currency:

\begin{lstlisting}
bool exactInput      = params.amountSpecified < 0;
bool unspecifiedIsEth = exactInput ? (!params.zeroForOne) : (params.zeroForOne);
if (!unspecifiedIsEth) return (BaseHook.afterSwap.selector, 0);
\end{lstlisting}

Enumerating the four quadrants:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quadrant} & \code{amountSpecified} & \code{zeroForOne} & \textbf{Charged?} \\
\midrule
exact-in buy & $<0$ & true & yes, in \code{\_beforeSwap} \\
exact-in sell & $<0$ & false & yes, in \code{\_afterSwap} \\
exact-out buy & $>0$ & true & yes, in \code{\_afterSwap} \\
\textbf{exact-out sell} & $>0$ & \textbf{false} & \textbf{no --- both skip it} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\code{\_beforeSwap} skips it for not being exact-input, and \code{\_afterSwap} returns
early because the unspecified leg is the \emph{token}. The swap executes completely
fee-free. Worse, the credit accrual in \code{\_afterSwap} runs \emph{before} that early
return, so the swapper still banks crystal credit and lifetime volume.

\textbf{Exploit path.} Sell by naming the ETH output instead of the token input --- a
one-word change, and a standard aggregator route. Measured on the fork: an exact-input
sell paid $4.24\times10^{15}$ wei; the identical exact-output sell paid \textbf{0}.

\textbf{Impact.} A total bypass of the protocol's only revenue mechanism on the sell side.
Every downstream sink is starved: the genesis dividend, the collection floor buy-back, the
relaunch reserve (which \emph{is} the next generation's liquidity) and the proposer
slice. Combined with the free credit accrual, a buy/sell round trip halves the intended
cost of minting the NFT collection.

\textbf{Fix (applied).} The quadrant is refused in \code{\_beforeSwap}. This is the
correct resolution rather than a workaround: v4's \code{afterSwapReturnDelta} applies to
the \emph{unspecified} currency, so an ETH fee physically cannot be taken on that leg, and
charging in token would contradict the hook's ETH-only fee design. Exact-input sells are
economically equivalent and unaffected.

\begin{lstlisting}
if (!exactInput && !params.zeroForOne) revert ExactOutSellUnsupported();
\end{lstlisting}

\finding{Z-02 --- The liquidation mark freezes whenever the perpetuals book is empty}
{\SevHigh}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Fixed}
{\code{PerpEngine.\_doSweep}, \code{\_writeObs}, \code{twapTick}}
{\code{test/attacks/Z02\_PerpStaleMark.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} The engine's TWAP oracle is written only by \code{\_pokeFunding}.
On the keeperless path --- the hook calling \code{sweepLiquidations} from
\code{afterSwap} --- that call sat \emph{after} an early return:

\begin{lstlisting}
if (_liqReentry) return;
uint256 len = _openIds.length;
if (len == 0) return;      // <-- returns BEFORE _pokeFunding()
_pokeFunding();            // the only keeperless oracle write
\end{lstlisting}

So while nothing is open, no swap ever samples the oracle. \code{lastTick} stays frozen at
the value it held when the book last emptied. \code{\_writeObs} then integrates the entire
quiet period at that stale tick (\code{tickCumulative += lastTick * dt}), and
\code{twapTick} extrapolates the same stale tick across the whole lookback. The mark
returns the pre-quiet-period price no matter how far spot has moved.

This is the same defect class the contract documents as fixed for the
\emph{intra-transaction} case; the inter-period case was still open because the oracle is
simply not sampled.

\textbf{Exploit path / measurement.} With an empty book, an 8~ETH buy moved spot from
$\sqrt{P}=4.4169\times10^{32}$ to $3.1822\times10^{32}$ (the token roughly doubling). One
hour later the mark still read $4.4168\times10^{32}$ --- unchanged. A fully collateralised
$2\times$ long opened at that moment was liquidated \emph{inside its own opening
transaction} by its own post-open sweep: \code{positions[id].trader == address(0)} and
\code{openCount == 0} immediately after a successful \code{openLong}.

\textbf{Impact.} Two directions. (i) The opener pays the 6.9\% liquidation penalty on a
position that was solvent at spot --- a direct, repeatable loss to honest users, and a
keeper reward plus Liquidatoor badge to whoever is watching. (ii) The mirror case: when
the stale mark is favourable, a genuinely insolvent position is \emph{un}liquidatable
until the mark converges, and the shortfall is charged to the ETH PLV.

\textbf{Fix (applied).} \code{\_pokeFunding()} is moved above the empty-book return, so
every served swap samples the oracle whether or not anything is open. This is what makes
the design genuinely keeperless; it costs one observation write on an otherwise idle
sweep. The regression suite asserts the mark now tracks spot to within 1\% after the same
one-hour quiet period, and that the position survives.

\finding{Z-03 --- Unbounded governor scan lets one NFT holder brick the rebirth}
{\SevHigh}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Fixed}
{\code{CauldronGovernor.\_recomputeLeader}, \code{\_bestUnconsumed}}
{\code{test/attacks/Z03\_GovernorSpamRelaunchDoS.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} \code{propose()} is permissionless for anyone holding one unit of
voting power, with no cooldown, deposit or per-address cap, and
\code{\_recomputeLeader} scanned \emph{every proposal ever submitted}.
\code{relaunch()} reaches that scan up to three times in a single call:

\begin{lstlisting}
CauldronRegistry.relaunch:597   governor.hasProposals()   -> _bestUnconsumed -> _recomputeLeader
CauldronRegistry.relaunch:660   governor.winner()         -> _bestUnconsumed -> _recomputeLeader
CauldronRegistry.relaunch:674   governor.markConsumed()   -> _recomputeLeader
\end{lstlisting}

The \code{markConsumed} recompute is unconditional on this path, because relaunch always
consumes exactly the id \code{winner()} returned --- i.e. the cached leader.

\textbf{Exploit path / measurement.} The scan cost grows strictly linearly: 5{,}239 gas at
1 proposal, 127{,}839 at 201, 495{,}639 at 801 --- a marginal \textbf{613 gas per spam
proposal, per scan}. Roughly 17{,}400 proposals exhaust a 32M budget across relaunch's

hundredths of an ETH. The leader cache does not save it: a voter can keep
\code{\_leaderId} pointing at a still-open proposal, which forced
\code{hasProposals()} from 1{,}464 gas to 196{,}766.

\textbf{Impact.} Permanent, permissionless halt of the eternal machine for a trivial cost.
It compounds badly with Z-06: \code{registry.setGovernor} is \code{onlyOwner}, and
ownership was burned into the presale, so a spammed governor could \emph{never} be
replaced. Recovery would have required a full V2 controller migration.

\textbf{Fix (applied).} \code{\_recomputeLeader} now scans at most
\code{MAX\_LEADER\_SCAN = 64} of the most recent proposals, making relaunch gas $O(1)$ in
the proposal count. The cached-leader fast path is untouched, so an established winner
keeps winning however much spam follows it, and a fresh mandate is by definition among the
most recent proposals. Post-fix the scan is flat: identical gas at 201 and 801 proposals.

\finding{Z-04 --- \code{rescueSeeder} permanently strands the active book and bricks rebirth}
{\SevHigh}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Fixed}

{\code{test/attacks/Z04\_SeederRescueStrandsLp.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} The documented break-glass for an ``aborted campaign'' returned only
the \emph{loose} balances and then disarmed the campaign:

\begin{lstlisting}
function rescue(address to) external onlyRegistry lock {
    ... transfer loose token + ETH ...
    seeding = false;                  // <-- placed positions left in the pool
}
\end{lstlisting}

Every registry path to the only recovery function is gated on that same flag

hatch was always destructive.

\textbf{Exploit path / measurement.} On a progressive generation seeded with 10~ETH, the
seeder held $1.534\times10^{23}$ liquidity units across three tracked ranges. After

\textbf{Impact.} Governance-triggerable, irreversible loss of an entire generation's active
liquidity, plus a permanent brick of the rebirth.

\textbf{Fix (applied).} \code{rescue} no longer clears \code{seeding}, so the registry's
teardown paths still reach \code{withdrawAll} and the book is recovered at the next
relaunch or handoff; and \code{startSeed} now performs \code{delete ranges} as part of its
per-campaign reset. The regression asserts that after a rescue the rebirth succeeds and
every gen-1 range is fully unwound.

\finding{Z-05 --- Block-denominated death clock destroys live tokens on the deployment target}
{\SevHigh}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Fixed}
{\code{CauldronHook.\_getCurrentBucket}, \code{\_recordVolume}, \code{getVolume24h}}
{\code{test/attacks/Z05\_L2BlockClock.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} The sliding volume window was counted in blocks, with constants
pinned to Ethereum's cadence:

\begin{lstlisting}
uint256 public constant BLOCKS_PER_HOUR = 300;   // "~12s blocks"
uint256 public constant BLOCKS_PER_DAY  = 7_200; // 300 * 24
function _getCurrentBucket() private view returns (uint256) {
    return (block.number / BLOCKS_PER_HOUR) % HOURS_PER_DAY;
}
\end{lstlisting}

As \S\ref{sec:l2} establishes, \code{block.number} on Nitro is the \emph{parent chain's}
block number. On an Orbit L3 settling to Arbitrum One ($\approx$0.25\,s), 7{,}200 blocks is
\textbf{30 minutes}, not 24 hours --- the death clock runs $48\times$ too fast.

\textbf{Exploit path.} \code{relaunch()} is permissionless and its only other gate,
\code{minLifetime}, is denominated in seconds. So: hold one MiFren, propose a brew, wait
out the 3-day voting period, then kill the live token during its first 30-minute lull once
it is an hour old. Demonstrated end to end by advancing \code{block.number} and
\code{block.timestamp} independently: with 7{,}201 blocks elapsed but only 61 minutes of
wall clock, a stranger's \code{relaunch()} succeeded and retired a healthy pool.

\textbf{Impact.} Permissionless, permanent destruction of a live, liquid token by an
unprivileged party --- forced LP teardown, forced perp closure, forced migration of every
holder. Both constants were \code{constant} (no setter, no migration path), and the hook
had 58 bytes of EIP-170 headroom, so one could not have been added post-deployment.
The pluggable \code{IDeathChecker} is only a partial mitigation, since the
\code{volume24h} figure it receives is itself computed over the mis-calibrated window.

\textbf{Fix (applied).} The window is re-denominated in wall-clock seconds
(\code{SECONDS\_PER\_HOUR} / \code{SECONDS\_PER\_DAY}), which carries the intended meaning
identically on L1, on an Orbit L2 and on an Orbit L3. To reclaim the necessary bytes, the
owner-only \code{trackPool} was removed --- itself a small security improvement, since it
let the owner hand-adopt a pool the registry never created, bypassing the
\code{\_afterInitialize} adoption gate. The hook now has 145 bytes of headroom.
Regressions assert that 7{,}201 blocks no longer kill a healthy token, that a stranger's
\code{relaunch()} reverts \code{TokenStillAlive}, and that 24 hours of genuine silence
still retires it.

try hook.forceClosePerps{gas: gasleft() - RELAUNCH_TAIL_RESERVE}() {} catch {}
\end{lstlisting}
and lower the \code{resolveTickets} budget inside relaunch. This was left unapplied because
it alters relaunch's failure semantics under partial drains and warrants its own
fork-tested change rather than being bundled with the security fixes.

\finding{Z-10 --- Depth-derived risk caps are flash-manipulable}
{\SevMed}{Likely --- no PoC}{\Residual}
{\code{PerpEngine.activeEthDepth}, \code{\_checkNotional}, \code{maxLeverage}, \code{\_tryLiquidate}}
{---}

\textbf{Description.} \code{activeEthDepth()} reads \code{poolManager.getLiquidity(id)} ---
the liquidity \emph{at the current tick}, an instantaneous quantity anyone can move by
minting or burning a concentrated position. Every risk limit derives from it: the
per-position notional cap (\code{maxNotionalBps}), the per-side OI cap
(\code{maxOiBps}), the leverage tier ladder, and the per-timestamp liquidation cap
(\code{maxLiqBps}).

\textbf{Exploit paths.} Two directions, both atomic from a contract (mint LP, act, burn LP
--- each in its own \code{unlock}):
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \emph{Inflate} depth to open a position far larger than the real book can absorb,
then withdraw the liquidity. The exit swap then slips badly; the trader loses their
collateral and the remainder is charged to insurance and then to LP principal.
\item \emph{Deflate} depth to a small non-zero value, shrinking
$\textit{cap} = \code{activeEthDepth} \times \code{maxLiqBps}/10^4$ below the position's
notional, so \code{liquidate} reverts \code{LiqCapped} and \code{\_tryLiquidate} silently
skips --- protecting an underwater position from liquidation for as long as the
manipulation is held. Note the guard is written \code{if (cap > 0 \&\& ...)}, so driving
depth to exactly zero \emph{disables} the check rather than blocking; the exploitable
region is small-but-non-zero.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Impact.} Bypass of the bounded-slippage assumption that underwrites ``no single
position can create bad debt'', and a temporary liquidation-evasion primitive. Not
directly profitable in isolation --- the manipulator bears the LP cost --- which is why this
is Medium rather than High.

\textbf{Recommendation (not applied).} Derive the caps from a time-averaged depth, or from
the protocol's own position sizes (which the registry knows exactly), rather than from
instantaneous in-range liquidity; and treat $\textit{cap} = 0$ as ``block'' rather than
``allow''.

\finding{Z-11 --- Queued PLV exits are senior to live shares}
{\SevMed}{Confirmed by construction}{\Residual}
{\code{PerpVault.withdrawEth}, \code{claimPendingEth}, \code{assetsEth}}
{---}

\textbf{Description.} A withdrawal that exceeds the free buffer is booked as a fixed claim
in \code{pendingEth} and subtracted from the share base:
$\code{assetsEth()} = \code{engine.totalEth()} - \code{pendingEth}$. The source documents
this as ``queued ETH \ldots{} stops bearing bad-debt risk'', which is precisely the problem:
\code{plv} can subsequently \emph{fall} via \code{\_absorbPlvLoss}, and the queued claim
does not. Live shares absorb the entire loss while queued claims are made whole first.

\textbf{Impact.} A first-mover advantage that rewards running: any LP who suspects bad debt
should withdraw immediately to convert into a senior fixed claim. In a stress event this is
a bank-run incentive, not merely an accounting quirk.

\textbf{Recommendation (not applied).} Either mark queued claims down pro-rata when
\code{\_absorbPlvLoss} fires, or keep them in the share base until actually paid. This is a
deliberate economic design decision rather than an implementation bug, so it is reported
for the protocol owners rather than unilaterally changed.

\finding{Z-12 --- Reserve solvency degrades silently at the legacy clamp}
{\SevMed}{Likely --- no PoC}{\Residual}
{\code{CauldronRegistry.relaunch} (steps 8, 8b)}
{---}

\textbf{Description.} The reserve backs three claimants at once: $1{:}1$ migration, the
genesis floor, and the collection legacy floors. Sizing is:

\begin{lstlisting}
uint256 newActive = tokensFromLP > unclaimedGenesis ? tokensFromLP - unclaimedGenesis : tokensFromLP;
if (newActive == 0) newActive = GEN1_ACTIVE_TOKENS;              // (a)
uint256 legacy = collectionLedger.totalEntitled();
newActive = newActive > legacy ? newActive - legacy : newActive;  // (b)
if (newActive > TOTAL_SUPPLY) newActive = TOTAL_SUPPLY;
uint256 newReserve = TOTAL_SUPPLY - newActive;
\end{lstlisting}

Branch (b) is the defect: when $\textit{legacy} \ge \textit{newActive}$ the subtraction is
skipped \emph{entirely} rather than clamped to zero, so the reserve is not enlarged for the
legacy claim at all. Branch (a) is worse in the degenerate case: a zero recovery
substitutes a fixed 80\% constant that bears no relation to the circulating supply needing
migration.

\textbf{Impact.} The three claimants compete first-come-first-served on an
under-collateralised pot. Late migrators hit \code{"reserve short"} and are permanently
unable to migrate $1{:}1$. Reachable after enough generations of accumulated entitlement, or
after an \code{emergencyWithdrawLP} has emptied a generation's LP.

\textbf{Recommendation (not applied).} Make the shortfall explicit and observable: emit a
diagnostic when the clamp binds, and prefer proportional haircutting across the three
claimants over silent first-come-first-served. Changing the allocation policy is an
economic decision for the protocol owners.

\finding{Z-14 --- \code{blockhash} seed expiry stalls the gacha queue on a fast parent chain}
{\SevMed}{Confirmed with PoC}{\Accepted}
{\code{CauldronHook.\_resolveTickets}, \code{CauldronCollection.reveal}, \code{MiFrensGenesis.reveal}}
{\code{test/attacks/Z05\_L2BlockClock.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} Ticket outcomes are seeded by \code{blockhash(commitBlock)}, valid
for 256 blocks. On an Orbit L3 that horizon is $\approx$64 seconds, so expiry becomes
routine rather than exceptional. The expiry path is:

\begin{lstlisting}
if (bh == 0) { b.commitBlock = uint48(block.number); break; }  // re-anchor, FIFO
\end{lstlisting}

Demonstrated: after 257 blocks, \code{resolveTickets(10)} processed \textbf{0} tickets and
silently re-anchored the batch.

\textbf{Impact.} Two effects. (i) The \code{break} head-of-line blocks the entire FIFO
queue behind one expired batch. (ii) The re-anchor is an additional draw for that batch's
owner. The design deliberately re-anchors rather than substituting a deterministic
fallback --- which is the right call, since a deterministic fallback would be a
\emph{predictable} second draw --- so the residual is a liveness and fairness wrinkle, not a
grinding vulnerability. The pity counter further limits the value of a re-roll.

\textbf{Disposition.} Accepted; recommend a keeper that resolves promptly, and consider
skipping past an expired batch rather than \code{break}ing so one stale entry cannot stall
the queue.

\finding{Z-17 --- \code{legacyBuyStep} settles its intended amount, not the actual delta}
{\SevLow}{Confirmed}{\Residual}
{\code{CauldronHook.legacyBuyStep}}
{---}

\textbf{Description.} The buy-back settles the constant \code{amt} rather than the swap's
realised debit:
\begin{lstlisting}
BalanceDelta d = poolManager.swap(key, SwapParams({... amountSpecified: -int256(amt) ...}), "");

\end{lstlisting}
If the price limit ever binds, the pool consumes less than \code{amt} and the hook

\code{legacyThreshold} (0.02~ETH by default) against a full-range book, so
\code{MIN\_SQRT\_LIMIT} cannot bind. Reported as a robustness defect and a latent hazard if
the threshold is ever raised or the book made much thinner.

\textbf{Recommendation (not applied).} Settle \code{uint256(uint128(-d.amount0()))} and
return any remainder to \code{legacyBuffer}. Deferred only because the hook's EIP-170
headroom was reserved for the Z-01 and Z-05 fixes.

wrong instinct, and the drift deserves to be recorded rather than silenced.

\section{Failed Attacks and Safety Arguments}\label{sec:failed}

Attacks we attempted and could \emph{not} make work. Each is recorded with the property
that defeats it, because these are the load-bearing guarantees.

\paragraph{SA-1 --- Shrinking the next generation's reserve by donating to the dying pool.}
\code{PoolOps.removeAll} collects accrued fees along with principal, so
\code{poolManager.donate()} inflates \code{tokensFromLP} and hence \code{newActive},
shrinking \code{newReserve} one-for-one. This looked like a cheap way to strand other
holders' migration. It is not, because the donation is conserved. Let $C$ be circulating
supply, $L$ the amount in the LP, $g$ the unclaimed genesis, with $C+L=\textit{TOTAL}$.
Then $\textit{newReserve} = \textit{TOTAL} - (L - g) = C + g$: exactly the circulating
supply needing migration, plus genesis. Donating $X$ moves $X$ from $C$ into $L$, giving
$C' = C-X$ and $\textit{newReserve}' = C-X+g$ --- still exact. The attacker destroys their
own tokens and strands nobody. The \code{newActive > TOTAL\_SUPPLY} clamp is therefore
defensive only; it cannot be reached without minting.

\paragraph{SA-2 --- Self-liquidation for profit.} A liquidator receives
$\code{keeperBps}=145$ bps \emph{of the penalty}, and the penalty is 6.9\% of collateral ---
so roughly 0.1\% of collateral is returned against a 6.9\% charge. Liquidating one's own
position is strictly loss-making. The Liquidatoor badge is the real incentive, and it is
not fungible.

\paragraph{SA-3 --- Guardian permanently disabling the break-glass.} A hostile guardian can
veto every armed action, apparently freezing emergency recovery forever. It cannot:
\code{setGuardian} is reachable by the \code{emergencyAdmin} and is \emph{not} itself
timelocked, so the timelock replaces the guardian and proceeds. The veto is genuinely
pure-upside.

\paragraph{SA-4 --- First-depositor inflation of the PerpVault.} Shares are minted with a
$10^6$ virtual offset, and --- decisively --- there is no permissionless path to inflate
\code{plv}: \code{fundPlv} is \code{onlyOwner}, \code{fundFromVault} is \code{onlyVault},
and \code{creditPerpFee} is hook-only. The only attacker-reachable inflation is routing
perp fees, which costs more than it moves.

\paragraph{SA-5 --- Forging the facet's return data or hijacking the delegatecall.}
\code{setRedemptionExt} is one-shot, rejects code-less targets (a \code{delegatecall} to an
empty account returns success with no data, which would make every redemption a silent
no-op), and the forwarder bubbles return data verbatim. The forwarders are deliberately
\emph{not} \code{nonReentrant} because the facet's own guard runs against the registry's
shared slot; adding one would double-lock. We confirmed the layouts are identical by
construction and that the remediation preserves this (INV-F).

\paragraph{SA-6 --- Vesting-grant spam to DoS a holder's \code{claim()}.}
\code{vestBatch} pulls $\min(\textit{balance}, \textit{allowance})$ in one go, so repeating
a victim's address within a batch yields zero on every subsequent iteration. Grant count is
bounded by the victim's own deposit actions.

\paragraph{SA-7 --- Double-voting across the L2 checkpoint granularity.} Because
\code{block.number} is coarse on Nitro, many transfers collapse into one OpenZeppelin
checkpoint, so we tested whether votes could be duplicated by shuffling NFTs inside a
parent-block window. They cannot: \code{vote} requires
\code{block.number > p.snapshot} and reads \code{getPastVotes} at that fixed snapshot, so
post-snapshot movement cannot mint voting power. The coarse clock only delays the opening
of voting by one parent block.

\paragraph{SA-8 --- Claiming an exempt tier by forging \code{hookData}.} A direct swapper
controls \code{hookData} freely, so we attempted to tag a tax-exempt address.
\code{\_isExemptPlayer} requires \code{isOpener[sender]} \emph{and}
\code{taxExempt[player]}, and \code{\_taxedPlayer} ignores the tag unless the sender is a
trusted opener. The exemption cannot be claimed by an untrusted caller.

\paragraph{SA-9 --- Sandwiching the hook's own buy-back.} The trigger
(\code{legacyBuffer >= legacyThreshold}) is publicly computable, and an attacker can trip it
with their own buy so the hook buys immediately afterwards at the inflated price. This
works, but the extractable value is bounded by the buffer (0.02~ETH by default), and the
mempool-less sequencer removes the classical variant. Recorded under Z-17 rather than as a
finding in its own right.

\paragraph{SA-10 --- Reaching the $69\times$ reserve through the seeder.}
\code{CauldronSeeder} custodies only what \code{startSeed} handed it and holds no reference
to the reserve position, which is a registry-owned \code{PositionManager} NFT. The
separation is structural, not procedural (INV SEED-1).

\finding{Y-01 --- Reserve-ceiling breach voids the exit guarantee above $\sim$69$\times$}
{\SevMed}{Confirmed with PoC}{\textcolor{med}{\textbf{Config + partial fix}}}
{\code{ReserveLib.reserveTicks}, \code{PoolOps.claimFromReserve}/\code{addToReserve}, \code{RedemptionExt.redeemOgFren}, \code{CauldronRegistry.claimByBurn}}
{\code{test/attacks/Y01\_ReserveCeilingBreach.t.sol}}

\textbf{Description.} The migration/genesis/legacy reserve is a single
\emph{out-of-range}, single-sided \code{token1} position placed \emph{below} the
launch tick (\code{tickUpper = alignDown(launchTick - offset)}, default
\code{offset = 42400} $\approx 69\times$). Every reserve-side operation assumes spot
stays \emph{above} \code{tickUpper} so the band is pure token:

\begin{lstlisting}
// claimFromReserve: "reserve is out of range so ETH out = 0" (amount1Min = 0)
uint128 liquidity = ReserveLib.liquidityForTokenOut(tickLower, tickUpper, amount);
// addToReserve: single-sided top-up, max0 (ETH) = 0
params[0] = abi.encode(positionId, liquidity, uint128(0), uint128(amount), "");
\end{lstlisting}

\code{liquidityForTokenOut} sizes $L$ across the \emph{whole} band. Once the token
appreciates past the ceiling and spot trades \emph{into} the band, removing that $L$
delivers only $\textit{amount}\cdot(\sqrt P-\sqrt{Lo})/(\sqrt{Hi}-\sqrt{Lo})$ of
token and returns the remainder as \emph{ETH}, so every claim short-delivers and
trips its own H-03 guard (\code{migrateOne}: \code{if (got + CLAIM\_DUST < amount)
revert}; \code{redeemOgFren}: \code{if (amount + 1e12 < F) revert NoBalance}), and
\code{addToReserve} reverts outright because it declares no ETH leg.

\textbf{PoC (all green on current code).} Launch tick $172{,}529$, ceiling
$\code{tickUpper}=130{,}000$; a $200$~ETH buy ($10\times$ the $20$~ETH seed) drives
spot to tick $128{,}279$. Then: \emph{(i)} \code{redeemOgFren} reverts for every
remaining OG while \code{floorPerFren()} still advertises the full
$1.5385\times10^{24}$; \emph{(ii)} \code{donateToReserve} (the floor-ratchet path)
reverts; \emph{(iii)} a gen-1 holder's \code{claimByBurn} reverts \code{"reserve
short"}. A one-spacing breach cannot leak the ETH leg (shortfall $\sim1.5\times
10^{22}$~wei, eleven orders above \code{CLAIM\_DUST}); and the next \code{relaunch}
self-heals by re-parking a fresh out-of-range reserve.

\textbf{Impact.} The advertised exit guarantee (1:1 migration + genesis floor) is
silently voided \emph{while} the token trades above its $69\times$ ceiling, exactly
when holders most want to exit, and the floor view keeps promising the full number.
Reachable by a plain buy (value not burned) or a genuine bull run. Bounded: it
self-heals at relaunch and cannot leak ETH.

\textbf{Fix.} \emph{(1) Config:} raise \code{nextReserveCeilingOffset} per iteration
via the existing \code{setReserveCeiling} (bounded $[4000,138000]$) so the ceiling
sits above any plausible appreciation --- a deeper ceiling costs only conversion
optics, not solvency. \emph{(2) Observability (APPLIED):}
\code{CauldronRegistry.floorClaimableNow()} returns \code{(bool claimable, uint256
perFren)} by comparing spot tick to the band's \code{tickUpper}, so the UI shows
``floor temporarily out of range'' rather than a bare revert; its signal matches
\code{redeemOgFren}'s reachability exactly (cost 299~B; registry $24{,}097$~B, 479
free). \emph{(3) Structural (owner call):} let the claim paths accept the ETH leg of
an in-range removal and credit it to the floor, degrading gracefully instead of
reverting.

\finding{Y-02 --- Depth-derived risk caps are flash-manipulable (resolves Z-10)}
{\SevMed}{Confirmed (mechanism); Refuted (drain)}{\textcolor{med}{\textbf{Bounded}}}

\textbf{Refuted as a drain (Y-02b) --- stronger than ``not profitable.''} The perp
token is single-sourced from the very pool whose depth is measured, so inflating
depth by factor $k$ costs token $\propto k$: the $4.6\times$ inflation above
consumed $3.64\times10^{25}$ tokens ($\approx4.7\%$ of the $7.77\times10^{26}$ fixed
supply) for a \emph{single} band. Cratering the true book to force bad debt needs an
order of magnitude more --- an unreachable fraction of supply --- so with a realistic
budget the PLV+insurance pot does not fall ($60.00\to60.09$~ETH). Direction-2
(``deflate depth to evade liquidation'') is structurally refuted for the green-candle
book: the protocol's full-range active LP is a hard floor on in-range depth that an
attacker cannot remove.

\textbf{Residual.} Time-averaged depth (the Z-10 recommendation) remains the right
hardening \emph{if} a future iteration seeds a thin, concentrated active book (e.g. a
progressive launch), where the scarcity bound weakens.

\section{Harness Defects Discovered During This Work}\label{sec:harness}

Two properties of this build profile silently invalidate time-dependent tests. Both had to
be corrected before Z-02 and Z-05 could be observed at all, and both are worth recording
because they would equally mislead future work on this repository.

\paragraph{H-1 --- \code{via\_ir} eliminates repeated \code{TIMESTAMP}/\code{NUMBER} reads.}
The \code{cauldron} profile compiles with \code{via\_ir = true}. Its optimiser treats the
\code{TIMESTAMP} and \code{NUMBER} opcodes as loop-invariant and
common-subexpression-eliminates them, so

\begin{lstlisting}
for (uint256 i = 0; i < 24; ++i) { vm.warp(block.timestamp + 150); engine.poke(); }
\end{lstlisting}

evaluates \code{block.timestamp} \emph{once} and warps to the same target 24 times. Measured
directly: 24 iterations advanced the clock by 150 seconds in total, and the engine's
\code{obsIndex} moved from 1 to 2 instead of to 25. Any test that advances a clock more
than once per function must hold the clock in a local or storage variable.

\paragraph{H-2 --- \code{vm.roll} re-snaps the timestamp on an unpinned fork.} On a fork
created without a pinned block, the block environment is re-synced behind the test, so
\code{vm.roll} overwrites a previously warped \code{block.timestamp}. Pinning
(\code{vm.createSelectFork(rpc, block.number - 64)}) makes the environment deterministic;
even then, \code{vm.roll} must not follow a \code{vm.warp} whose elapsed time is under
test.

\medskip\noindent
The practical consequence for this codebase is worth stating plainly: under H-1, a test
written to verify that the permissionless \code{poke()} keeps the perpetuals oracle fresh
would appear to pass while never advancing the clock at all. That is precisely the shape of
Z-02.
